\begin{notebox}
\textbf{\sffamily Read this first.} Build in the order given, and test at the end
of every stage. Each stage adds one subsystem and finishes with a command that
proves it.

\vspace{1ex}
Wiring everything and then powering on means debugging five subsystems at once
\textemdash{} and three of these five fail \emph{silently}. A servo on the wrong
pin, a ring that never lights, and an RTC with a dead cell all look identical
from the outside: a machine sitting there doing nothing.
\end{notebox}

\section{How a dose happens}
\label{sec:model}

Read this before you wire anything. It is the one thing about this machine that
is not obvious from its parts, and every stage that follows makes more sense
once you have it.

\vspace{1mm}
\noindent\textbf{\sffamily The dispenser asks before it drops.} At the scheduled
minute nothing leaves the hopper. The ring goes green, the buzzer starts
chirping, and a release button appears on the app's main screen \textemdash{}
also as a push notification, so the phone does not have to be open. The tray
opens when somebody taps it.

\vspace{1mm}
\noindent Two consequences worth knowing before you build:

\vspace{2mm}
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedright}p{44mm} >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{102mm}@{}}
\toprule
\hdr{What changes} & \hdr{Why it is built this way} \\
\midrule
\textbf{Nothing is ever dispensed unattended} &
Pills only leave the machine when somebody has said they are there to take
them. A dose nobody asks for stays in the hopper, where the next person to look
can still count it \textemdash{} rather than in a cup that may or may not have
been emptied by somebody who then takes the next dose as well \\
\textbf{The buzzer is not optional any more} &
It used to announce pills that had already dropped. Now it is the \emph{prompt}:
a machine that cannot chirp waits out the hour in silence and records a miss.
Stage~2 fits it early for that reason \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\vspace{2mm}
\begin{notebox}
\textbf{\sffamily There is no button on this machine.} Earlier builds had a
momentary switch on GPIO 24 for confirming a dose after it dropped. It is gone,
along with its two wires: the tap that releases the dose is the same tap that
records it, so a second control confirming the first had nothing left to say.
GPIO 24 is now free.
\end{notebox}

\vspace{1mm}
\begin{warnbox}
\warntitle{The release has to be able to reach the device \textemdash{} check you have one of the two paths}
On the house WiFi the phone talks to \code{pilldispenser.local} directly, and no
internet is involved. Away from the house it goes through the relay
(Stage~8). A machine with neither working will chirp for an hour and then
record a missed dose, which is safe and is not useful.

\vspace{1ex}
\code{-{}-selftest} checks for both and fails if there is no path at all.
\end{warnbox}

\section{Powering up and down between stages}
\label{sec:power}

Every stage has the same rhythm, and it is worth saying once rather than
repeating it eleven times.

\vspace{2mm}
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[
  ph/.style={rounded corners=2pt, draw=ink40, fill=white, line width=0.7pt,

  \node[on]  (b) at (3.9,0)  {\textbf{plug in}\\[-0.3ex]wait for it to boot};
  \node[on]  (c) at (7.8,0)  {\textbf{prove it}\\[-0.3ex]run the stage's command};

    \draw[-{Stealth[length=2.2mm]}, ink40, line width=0.8pt] (\x) -- (\y);}

       (d.south) -- ++(0,-0.55) -| (a.south);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\vspace{1mm}
\noindent So: \textbf{power off to wire, power on to test.} The stages below do
not repeat the instruction \textemdash{} they say what to connect, and then what
to run once it is back on.

\vspace{2mm}
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedright}p{40mm} >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{106mm}@{}}
\toprule
\hdr{Power state} & \hdr{What you may do} \\
\midrule
\textbf{Unplugged} &
Connect or move any wire. Fit or remove the coin cell. Mount a servo horn \\
\textbf{Running} &
Run the \code{curl} commands, watch the ring, listen to the buzzer, meter the
servo rail. \textbf{Do not move a wire on a running Pi} \textemdash{} a signal
pin brushed against 5\,V is a dead GPIO, and a ground momentarily lifted while a
servo is drawing current is a brown-out \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\vspace{2mm}
\begin{notebox}
\textbf{\sffamily ``Unplug'' means unplug.} Not a shutdown that leaves the 5\,V
rail live, and not a hub switch. The Pi's GPIO pins are powered whenever the
board is, and \code{sudo halt} does not change that.

\vspace{1ex}
The one exception is the servo supply, which is a separate plug: it can be left
off through every stage up to Stage~4, and that is the safer way to work.
\end{notebox}

\vspace{-2mm}
\begin{warnbox}
\warntitle{The DS1302 keeps worse time than you would expect}
Its 32.768\,kHz crystal is uncompensated, so a typical module drifts a few
minutes a month, and more as the room warms and cools. That is good enough for
the job it has here \textemdash{} carrying the time across a power cut until the
Pi can reach an NTP server again \textemdash{} and it is \emph{not} good enough
as the only time source on a device that never sees a network. Commission this
build on a network and leave it able to re-sync.

\vspace{1.5ex}
\textbf{Fit the cell before you set the time}, because what the chip remembers
with the power off is the whole point of it. Most modules ship with a holder
marked LIR2032; an ordinary non-rechargeable CR2032 is the safer choice and
works, because the DS1302's built-in trickle charger stays switched off here.
\end{warnbox}

\section{The header, and every pin this build touches}
\label{sec:header}

Keep this page open while you wire. Everything is BCM numbering, which is what
both GPIO libraries use; the small number inside each pad is the \emph{physical}
pin, which is what you actually count on the board.

\vspace{3mm}
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=1.0]
  \drawheader[0.60]
\end{tikzpicture}

\vspace{4mm}
\headerlegend
\end{center}

\vspace{4mm}
\begin{multicols}{2}
\noindent\textbf{\sffamily The pins that were avoided, and what each costs}

\vspace{1ex}
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedright}p{20mm} >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{48mm}@{}}
\toprule
\hdr{Pin} & \hdr{Why it is not used} \\
\midrule
GPIO 0, 1 & HAT ID EEPROM. Driving them is how a HAT you plug in later fails to be detected \\
GPIO 2, 3 & Permanent 1.8\,k\textohm{} pull-ups. A servo line idling high slams the horn at power-on, and on the RTC's bidirectional data line a \emph{disconnected} wire would read as valid data \\
GPIO 14, 15 & The serial console: the one way back into a Pi whose network setup you just broke \\
GPIO 7--11 & SPI. Free today, gone the moment anyone adds an SPI display \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\vspace{1ex}
\noindent GPIO 24 and 25 are free and unassigned. GPIO 24 carried the confirm
button on earlier builds and has nothing on it now.

\columnbreak

\noindent\textbf{\sffamily The eight tray pins}

\vspace{1ex}
\footnotesize
GPIO 17, 27, 22, 5, 6, 13, 19 and 26, in tray order. All eight are checked for
clashes and against the pins that cost hardware, and the firmware accepts all
eight out of the box \textemdash{} so however many hoppers this machine has
today, the next one is \textbf{a plug}: no config edit, no restart with a
different number.

\vspace{1ex}
If you would rather the device \emph{refuse} a schedule for a tray that is not
physically there, say how many are fitted:

\vspace{1ex}
\begin{shell}
sudo systemctl edit snapcare-dispenser     # add: Environment=SNAPCARE_TRAYS=2
sudo systemctl restart snapcare-dispenser
\end{shell}

\noindent Set it in the unit rather than on a command line. Running a second
copy of the firmware by hand on a commissioned device fails anyway
\textemdash{} port 80 is already taken by the service \textemdash{} and an
environment variable typed at a shell does not survive the next reboot, which is
the moment it stops being true.

\vspace{1ex}
\code{tests/test\_servo.py} asserts all of this, and that no peripheral shares a
pin with a servo.
\end{multicols}

\begin{notebox}
\textbf{\sffamily This is not a bus, and there is no kernel driver.} The DS1302
is three plain GPIOs shifted by hand in software, so \code{i2cdetect},
\code{/dev/rtc0} and \code{hwclock} have nothing to say about it \textemdash{}
the firmware is the only thing that can read this chip, and \code{/api/status}
is where you look.

\vspace{1ex}
All three of its lines are deliberately kept off GPIO 2 and 3, whose permanent
1.8\,k\textohm{} pull-ups are wrong here in two separate ways. On the
bidirectional \code{I/O} line the pull-up sources current the chip has to sink
to show a zero \textemdash{} and a \emph{floating} line then reads as a one, so
a disconnected wire would look exactly like valid data. On the clock line it
holds SCLK high before the pin is configured, breaking the chip's rule that SCLK
must be low when CE rises.
\end{notebox}

\begin{provebox}
\provetitle
\begin{shell}
curl -s http://localhost/api/status     # the "clock" object: trusted, source, rtc
curl -X POST http://localhost/api/clock \
     -H 'Content-Type: application/json' -d '{"iso":"2026-07-29T09:15:00"}'
\end{shell}
First check the chip was actually written. \code{clock.rtc.detail} must not say
``no record of ever having been set'' \textemdash{} if it does, the write did
not reach the chip and there is no point going on to the power cut.

\vspace{1ex}
Then prove the only thing the chip is there for. \textbf{Pull the plug}, leave
it a minute, and bring it back with the network unplugged: \code{trusted} must
still be true and \code{source} must read \code{rtc}. A module that reads back
the time you just set, but loses it across a power cut, has a dead cell or a
cell holder that is not making contact.
\end{provebox}

\begin{warnbox}
\warntitle{\code{sudo shutdown now} does not test the coin cell}
It halts the operating system and leaves the board powered: the 5\,V and 3.3\,V
rails stay live, so the DS1302 keeps running on \emph{your} 3.3\,V from pin 17
and never falls back to its cell. The test passes and proves nothing.

\vspace{1ex}
Pull the power lead out. That is the only thing that puts the chip on its
battery, which is the entire question this stage is asking.
\end{warnbox}

\begin{warnbox}
\warntitle{This chip cannot answer the question the firmware asks}
Worth understanding before trusting it, because it is the one place the DS1302
is a real step down from the DS3231 the Arduino build used.

\vspace{1.5ex}
A DS3231 has an \emph{oscillator stop flag}: the hardware sets it whenever the
oscillator has stopped, and it stays set until software clears it. That answers
the only question worth asking \textemdash{} \emph{did this chip lose time at
some point, so that everything it has said since is invented?}

\vspace{1.5ex}
\textbf{The DS1302 has no such bit.} Its clock-halt flag is a control, not a
status: nothing in the hardware ever sets it, and its power-on state is
documented as undefined. A clear flag means only ``running right now'', which is
equally true of a chip that sat unpowered for a month and resumed counting from
whatever was left in its registers.

\vspace{1.5ex}
So the firmware reconstructs the evidence instead. The DS1302 has 31 bytes of
RAM in the \emph{same battery-backed domain as the oscillator} \textemdash{} the
coin cell holds both or neither. Setting the clock writes a token there, and on
boot that token is present if and only if the cell has held continuously since.
It is firmware convention rather than a hardware guarantee, and both of its
failure modes fall towards \emph{not} trusting the clock, which is the safe
direction. \code{snapcare\_dispenser/ds1302.py} is the whole argument.
\end{warnbox}

\begin{warnbox}
\warntitle{Nothing but this firmware ever sets the clock}
On a DS3231 the kernel reads the chip and sets the system clock before userspace
starts. The DS1302 has no such path on a Pi at all, so the firmware reads it
itself at startup \textemdash{} which happens later, after systemd has already
timestamped its own boot. That is the cost of the part.

\vspace{1.5ex}
Two things follow. \code{i2cdetect}, \code{/dev/rtc0} and \code{hwclock} have
nothing to say about this chip, so \code{/api/status} is the only place to look;
and \code{fake-hwclock} is no longer competing with a kernel RTC, so leaving it
installed is harmless.
\end{warnbox}

\begin{dangerbox}
\dangertitle{Do not run the servos from the Pi's 5\,V pin}
One SG90 stalls at around 700\,mA. The failure is not a blown regulator; it is a
brown-out, and \textbf{a Pi that browns out mid-write corrupts an SD card}
\textemdash{} where the file being written is the one that records whether a
dose has already been given.
\end{dangerbox}

\noindent The common ground is what makes the signal mean anything. Without it
the servos twitch or ignore commands entirely, which reads as a firmware fault
and is not one. The bulk capacitor absorbs the current step when a servo starts,
which is what otherwise dips the rail.

\begin{provebox}
\provetitle
Meter across the rail. \textbf{5\,V $\pm$0.25\,V, steady.} Nothing else is
connected yet, so anything else is the supply or the wiring, not the firmware.
\end{provebox}

\vspace{1mm}
If a tray does not quite reach its stop, or buzzes at the end of the stroke
because it is being driven past it, trim \emph{that tray alone}:

\begin{pycode}
SERVO_TRIM = {3: (520, 2450)}    # config.py -- overrides only tray 3
\end{pycode}

\begin{warnbox}
\warntitle{Buzzing at the end of a stroke is the one to act on}
It is the servo fighting a mechanical stop it has already reached, and it is
what burns one out over a few weeks.
\end{warnbox}

\noindent The other knob, if a full hopper is slower than an empty one:

\begin{pycode}
SERVO_STEP_S = 1.0     # seconds between one command in the stroke and the next
\end{pycode}

\noindent This is the whole timing model. Nothing on an SG90 reports position,
so the firmware is simply waiting out the clock, and the interval must cover the
\emph{slowest} case \textemdash{} a cold servo under the weight of a full tray,
about half a second \textemdash{} and still leave the hopper time to settle
before the horn reverses. Raising it lengthens the drop by four times whatever
you add.

\vspace{2mm}
\begin{provebox}
\provetitle
This is the first point at which the whole machine can be run end to end.
Restart the service, schedule a dose a minute or two out, and \emph{do not touch
anything}:

\begin{shell}
sudo systemctl start snapcare-dispenser
curl -s http://localhost/api/status      # read "now", then pick a time 2 min later
curl -X POST http://localhost/api/schedule \
     -H 'Content-Type: application/json' -d '{"payload":"SC1*W*1:HHMM-127-000"}'
\end{shell}

\noindent At \code{HHMM} the ring should go green and the buzzer should start
chirping, and \textbf{no tray should open}. Then release it:

\begin{shell}
curl -s http://localhost/api/status | grep -i awaitingRelease
curl -X POST http://localhost/api/release -H 'Content-Type: application/json' -d '{}'
\end{shell}

\noindent Now the tray sweeps and the ring goes blue. If you would rather see
the other branch, leave it alone for the full grace hour: the tray must stay
shut and the ring must go amber.
\end{provebox}

\begin{dangerbox}
\dangertitle{The test nobody does: unplug the internet, not the device}

release a due dose from the app. The pill must still come out.

\vspace{1ex}
This matters more than it did before the release model. A build that has just
gained a cloud feature is exactly when a dependency on it gets introduced by
accident \textemdash{} and now that dependency would not merely delay a report,
it would mean the tray cannot be opened at all. The symptom is a machine that
works perfectly in every test anyone runs at a desk.
\end{dangerbox}

\begin{notebox}
\textbf{\sffamily A release is not a schedule, and the relay carries it
differently.} The schedule lives in a slot the device treats as \emph{state}:
whatever is newest is what it should be running, so a device coming back after a
week adopts it. A release is an \emph{act} \textemdash{} true for one hour on one
morning \textemdash{} so it has a slot of its own, and the device carries one out
only if the dose it names is one it is offering right now.

\vspace{1ex}
That is what stops a release tapped at 08:05 and delivered at 3\,p.m., by a
relay that was down in between, from dropping a pill into an empty room.
\end{notebox}

\section{Stage 9 \textemdash{} the label}

The setup code is printed once and never changes. It is derived, not generated:
HMAC-SHA256 over the SoC serial in \code{/proc/cpuinfo}, so it survives a
reimage and differs per board. The pairing code on it comes from the same
derivation, so it is permanent in the same way.

\begin{shell}
sudo python3 -m snapcare_dispenser --show-qr      # scannable, in the terminal

\begin{dangerbox}
\dangertitle{The pairing code is a credential}
Anyone who can read it can send this device a schedule \emph{and release a
dose}, in the same way anyone who can read the WiFi password can join the
hotspot. Put the label where the owner can find it and a visitor cannot
\textemdash{} inside a battery door, or on the underside.
\end{dangerbox}

\begin{warnbox}
\warntitle{The one part of the label that is not permanent}
The pairing QR encodes \emph{your relay's address} alongside the code, because
that is what makes a phone camera able to open it. Move to a different relay and
that QR points at the old one. The code printed beside it in text still works
\textemdash{} it is derived from the board, not from the relay \textemdash{} so
the fix is to reprint, not to re-pair. If you expect to move relays, print the
label with no relay configured: the QR then carries the code alone, which the
app can read and a plain camera cannot.
\end{warnbox}

\section{Commissioning}

Run this before the device goes anywhere near real medication.

\begin{shell}
sudo systemctl stop snapcare-dispenser
sudo python3 -m snapcare_dispenser --selftest
sudo systemctl start snapcare-dispenser
\end{shell}

\noindent It checks what can be checked, asks about what only eyes and ears can
settle, and writes a report to \code{/var/lib/snapcare/commissioning.json}. It
opens trays, so it says so and waits for you to agree first.

\vspace{2mm}
\begin{notebox}
\textbf{\sffamily Why this is not a paper checklist any more.} The list this
replaced was a good list with a structural problem: most of its claims were not
things a person can actually observe.

\vspace{1ex}
``Servos silent and cool a few seconds after a drop'' is a real failure with a
real consequence and it is unanswerable three stages later, so the self-test
waits three seconds after the last stroke and asks then. ``Every tray you fitted
moves'' is ticked truthfully by a machine whose trays 1 and 2 are wired to each
other's pins \textemdash{} both moved \textemdash{} so the self-test asks
\emph{which hopper moved}, by number. And ``the buzzer works'' is ticked by an
active buzzer playing the wrong note, so it asks whether you heard a tone or a
click.

\vspace{1ex}
The rule it follows throughout: never ask about something it did not just make
happen.
\end{notebox}

\vspace{2mm}
\noindent It reports in four states, and the last is the one worth reading:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}l >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{104mm}@{}}
\toprule
\hdr{} & \hdr{} \\
\midrule
\code{ok} & Checked and correct \\
\code{FAIL} & Checked and wrong, with the fix beside it \\
\code{warn} & True but not blocking \textemdash{} a Python-timed servo, a tray count left at the default \\
\code{skip} & \textbf{Nobody looked.} ``Everything passed'' and ``everything anybody looked at passed'' are different sentences, and this is the difference \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\vspace{3mm}
\noindent\textbf{\sffamily Six things it cannot do, which are the six that
matter most.} Each needs the power, the network or the calendar interfered with,
and each is invisible in any test run at a desk:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}>{\raggedright}p{34mm} >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{110mm}@{}}
\toprule
\hdr{Test} & \hdr{What it catches} \\
\midrule
\textbf{The power cut} &
Pull the power at 08:30, after the 08:00 dose has been released, and restore it.
No second dose, and the log survives. This is exactly the case that dispenses
twice without persisted state \\
\textbf{The coin cell} &
Unplug the Pi, leave it a minute, bring it back with the network unplugged.
\code{clock.trusted} must still be true and \code{clock.source} must read
\code{rtc}. Nothing on a bench with WiFi proves this, because NTP vouches first
and the RTC is never consulted \\
\textbf{The dose nobody takes} &
Let a scheduled dose sit for its whole grace window without releasing it. The
tray must stay shut, the log must say missed, and the ring must go amber. A
build that quietly drops it at the end has lost the point of asking first \\
\textbf{The stale notification} &
Let a dose expire, then tap release on a notification still sitting on the
phone's lock screen. Nothing must move \textemdash{} the device's own sense of
what is due is the authority, and this is the only way to watch it refuse \\
\textbf{The internet cut} &
Pull the router's WAN cable, leave the dispenser and the phone on the LAN, and
release a due dose. It must come out. This catches a release path that only
works through the relay, which passes every test at a desk \\
\textbf{The wrong password} &
Type the WiFi password wrong on purpose. The setup network must come back within
a few seconds and the page you re-open must say the password was not accepted \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\vspace{2mm}
\noindent And two more if the machine will be left with somebody:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, label=\chk, itemsep=0.55ex, topsep=0.4ex]
\item Change the router's password, reboot the device, wait: the setup hotspot
      appears on its own rather than the device sitting there unreachable
\item Power the router and the device off together and bring only the device
      back for a few minutes first. It raises its hotspot, then finds the router
      by itself once that returns \textemdash{} no hands
\end{itemize}

\section{When it does not work}

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{longtable}{@{}>{\raggedright}p{56mm} >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{92mm}@{}}
\toprule
\hdr{Symptom} & \hdr{Look at} \\
\midrule
\endhead
\multicolumn{2}{@{}l}{\textbf{\sffamily Releasing a dose}}\\
The ring goes green and chirps, but no release button appears in the app &
The app has not heard about the dose. On the LAN, check \code{pilldispenser.local}
resolves from the phone; through the relay, check \code{cloud.state} is
\code{connected}. \code{device.awaitingRelease} in \code{/api/status} is what the
app is waiting to see \\
Tapping release does nothing, and the ring stays green &
\code{device.blockedReason} says which of the two gates it is: an untrusted
clock, or no GPIO library. They need opposite fixes \\
Tapping release says nothing was waiting &
The window closed \textemdash{} \code{GRACE\_MINUTES}, one hour by default. The
dose is in the log as \code{not-released}. This is correct behaviour, not a
fault \\
Doses expire untaken every day &
Nobody is being told. Almost always a missing buzzer: check \code{gpio.missing}.
On this build the chirp is the prompt, not an announcement \\
The release works at home and not away &
That is the LAN path working and the relay path not. Stage 8, and
\code{cloud.state} \\
\midrule
\multicolumn{2}{@{}l}{\textbf{\sffamily Servos}}\\
Servo twitches or ignores commands & Common ground. The Pi's GND and the servo supply GND must be joined \\
Servo hums between doses & You are on the RPi.GPIO fallback \textemdash{} \code{sudo apt install python3-lgpio}. \code{gpio.servo.timing} in \code{/api/status} says which you have: \code{dma}, \code{threaded} or \code{python} \\
Nothing moves and no error appears & No GPIO library at all. The firmware refuses to dispense rather than logging doses that never came out \textemdash{} \code{gpio.missing} says so. \code{sudo apt install python3-lgpio} \\
\code{apt install pigpio} says there is no such package & Correct, on Raspberry Pi OS Trixie. It is not coming back; lgpio is the driver now \\
Servo buzzes at the end of a stroke & Being driven past its mechanical stop. Trim it in \code{SERVO\_TRIM} before it burns out \\
Pi reboots when a servo moves & Servos are on the Pi's 5\,V pin, or the rail has no bulk capacitor \\
\midrule
\multicolumn{2}{@{}l}{\textbf{\sffamily Ring and buzzer}}\\
Ring never lights, DMA error in the log & Data line is not on GPIO 18/12/21/10 \\
First pixel is the wrong colour & 330\ohm{} missing, or at the Pi end instead of the ring end. If it persists, the ring's 5\,V logic threshold ($0.7 \times V_{DD} = 3.5$\,V) is above the Pi's 3.3\,V output \textemdash{} shorten the lead, or drop the ring's supply to $\sim$4.5\,V \\
Ring flickers only when a servo moves & pigpio and the ring contending for PWM. pigpiod's \code{-t} selects its clock peripheral: \code{-t 1} is PCM, which is the default and the one that leaves PWM0 free for the ring. \code{-t 0} selects PWM and is the setting that \emph{causes} this \\
Buzzer makes a tone but not \emph{this} tone & It is an active buzzer with its own oscillator. Use a passive element \\
Ring is cyan and stays cyan & The device is on its own setup hotspot waiting to be put on a network. Join \code{SnapCare-\ldots} and the page opens itself \\
\midrule
\multicolumn{2}{@{}l}{\textbf{\sffamily Clock and schedule}}\\
Clock right at shutdown, wrong at boot & The cell is dead or not seated. \code{clock.rtc.detail} distinguishes ``never been set'' from ``the cell has been flat since'' \\
\code{clock.rtc} says ``no record of ever having been set'', and still does after a reboot & The chip has never been written. Check \code{journalctl -u snapcare-dispenser | grep -i "clock chip"} \textemdash{} a seed is logged whenever NTP first synchronises and every \code{RTC\_RESEED\_S} after that. If nothing appears, no trustworthy clock has been available to write down \\
The RTC never changes after a \code{shutdown} and a boot & A shutdown leaves the board powered, so the chip never ran on its cell and nothing was being tested. Pull the plug instead \\
Clock drifts a few minutes a month & Expected \textemdash{} the DS1302's crystal is uncompensated. Leave the device able to reach NTP \\
A schedule is refused by tray number & This build was told how many trays it has. Raise \code{SNAPCARE\_TRAYS}, or drop it to use all eight \\
A dose for a tray that was never fitted is never offered, and nothing complains & All eight are accepted by default and an SG90 reports nothing. Set \code{SNAPCARE\_TRAYS} to the number really fitted \\
A schedule POST answers 421 & The \code{Host} header is not a name this device answers to. Use \code{pilldispenser.local} or the IP; \code{SNAPCARE\_EXTRA\_HOSTS} adds a router's own domain \\
\midrule
\multicolumn{2}{@{}l}{\textbf{\sffamily Network and cloud}}\\
\code{pilldispenser.local} does not resolve & avahi, and the hostname. The web app's \code{DEFAULT\_HOST} is that exact string \\
Setup page says ``did not hand out an address'' & The password was right and DHCP was not. Usually a router that needs restarting, or a MAC filter \\
The setup hotspot keeps disappearing for half a minute & That is the unattended retry finding a saved network worth trying. It stops once one works; raise \code{HOTSPOT\_RETRY\_S} if it is in the way of a bench session \\
Setup page never leaves ``waiting\ldots'' & The phone has not gone back to the house WiFi. Switch it over; the page follows. If the device's own network reappeared, the password was refused \\
Cloud \code{state: unsupported} & \code{sudo apt install python3-cryptography}. Everything else still works meanwhile \\
Cloud \code{state: retrying}, DNS in \code{lastError} & The Pi has no working resolver. It joined the WiFi but got no DNS, or the router blocks it \\
Cloud connects, but no schedule ever arrives & The app is paired to a different code. Compare \code{cloud.deviceId} here with the one the app shows \textemdash{} they must match exactly \\
The app shows the device as never seen & Nothing has written to the mailbox. Check \code{reportedRev} here is 1 or more \\
A schedule sent from the app is refused & \code{cloud.lastError} says why, in the same words the LAN API would use. Usually a tray that is not fitted \\
Cloud fine, but doses take minutes to appear in the log & \code{SNAPCARE\_CLOUD\_IDLE\_S} is set. That is the cheap mode working as asked \textemdash{} it does not delay releases, which are polled continuously either way \\
\bottomrule
